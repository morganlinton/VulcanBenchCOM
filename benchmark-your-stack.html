For engineering teams

Your API bill is a guess. Measure it.

Most teams pay one frontier model, at high or max effort, for every task. A stack benchmark rebuilds your work as an eval suite, runs it across models and effort levels, and tells you what each class of task actually needs, with the files to enforce it.

Companies are paying Anthropic and OpenAI hundreds of thousands of dollars a year at API prices, and inside those companies the pattern is almost always the same: one model from each lab, set to high or max effort, for everything from a one-line bug fix to a cross-service migration. Nobody has measured whether the top setting earns its price on that work. The public reports say it often doesn’t: frontier accuracy has converged while cost still spreads by an order of magnitude, and raising effort helps one model and hurts another on identical tasks. On your stack, the answer is unknown. That is the only problem this engagement solves.

Why the default is expensive

The best model isn’t a model. It’s a table.

The public reports keep landing on the same three findings. Frontier accuracy has largely converged while cost still spreads by an order of magnitude (Report 03). Reasoning effort pays for one model and backfires for another on the identical tasks (Report 05, and the effort curves on the leaderboard). And when tasks are run repeatedly, the cheapest model can be the most dependable one (Report 06).

Put those together and “which model is best” stops being a useful question. The useful one is which model, at which effort, for which class of task. A public leaderboard can’t answer that for you, because your stack and your task mix aren’t generic. It has to be measured on your work.

What you get
01

A routing policy, as files

A model-and-effort policy per task class (routine fixes, tests, feature work, refactors, novel work), with escalation rules for when the cheap route misses. Delivered as a config your platform team drops into whatever gateway or router you run, with a README. Not a slide.

02

Your own eval suite

Built from your merged PRs, or matched equivalents: your languages, your codebase scale, your task mix, with hidden tests that fail before the fix and pass after. It stays private, and it’s yours to rerun when the next model ships.

03

The cost model

Your task volume priced through the policy against what you pay today, break-even per task class, re-runnable. This is the number you take to whoever signs the invoice, alongside a private report in the same format as the public ones.

How it works
1

I learn your stack

Languages, frameworks and versions, the size and shape of your codebases, and the task mix: bug fixes, feature work, refactors, test writing, migrations, plus volume and latency constraints. Per team if your teams don’t share a stack; a platform team on Go and a product team on TypeScript get their own suite and their own table.

2

I build the suite to match it

Evals from your own merged pull requests where possible, curated equivalents where not: same language, same scale of codebase, same kind of change. Every task gets hidden tests validated to fail before the fix and pass after, the same standard as the public suite. Your code never becomes public eval material.

3

The matrix runs

Models × effort levels × repeats, in the network-isolated Docker sandbox with deterministic grading and no judges. Per task class: pass@1, pass^k reliability, tokens, cost, and wall-clock, the same view the public reports use.

4

You get the policy and the cost model

The matrix collapses into a routing table with escalation rules, shipped as config plus the report and a walkthrough with your team. When a new model ships, the suite already exists; rerunning the matrix is the cheap part.

Where the line is
Private by default, vendor-neutral by design

A stack benchmark is your code, your suite, your numbers: tasks built from your repositories never become public evals and never appear on the leaderboard. No model vendor pays for placement in a routing recommendation. The policy is bought by you, graded by hidden tests, and owes nothing to anyone whose model appears in it.

What it costs

Less than one month of the bill it audits.

A focused engagement, one work profile and a handful of task classes across a four-model matrix, starts in the mid four figures. Against a six-figure annual API spend, a single reclassified task class usually pays for it. Broader matrices and bigger suites cost more; per-team suites add a suite build, not a whole new engagement. Compute is metered and shown, the same cost transparency as the public reports, and reruns on an existing suite are a fraction of the initial build. You pay for the measurement, never for a verdict.
